Yerukula Community

The Yerukula — also written Yerukala and Erukula — are one of the principal Scheduled Tribes of Andhra Pradesh. Once a semi-nomadic people of the Nallamala forests and the plains beyond, they carry a distinct Dravidian dialect, an occupational sub-tribe system, four gotras and a strong kula panchayat. This site records that heritage — including the story of their forced settlement at Stuartpuram and their long journey toward dignity.

History of the Yerukula

The name Yerukula comes from the Telugu word Yeruka — foresight, or the disclosing of things past, present and future. The word Yerukalachepputa means fortune-telling, and it was the Yerukula women who practised it. That single profession gave the whole community its name.

The Yerukula speak a Dravidian dialect of their own — variously called Yerukula Basha, Kurru or Yerulaki — described in the older records as an admixture of Tamil, Telugu and Kannada, and in the Godavari region as carrying Konkani influences as well. Most Yerukulas also speak Telugu and can communicate freely outside their own group.

They are found across Andhra Pradesh — chiefly in Krishna, Guntur, Kurnool, Anantapur, Nellore, Prakasam, Chittoor and Kadapa districts, and in the Godavari districts — and beyond, in Tamil Nadu, Karnataka and Kerala, where closely related communities go by the names Korava, Koracha / Korama and Kaikadi.

From Traders to “Criminal Tribe”

The Yerukula were originally grain and salt traders who operated across the Madras Presidency. Their descent into poverty and crime was a direct consequence of British economic policies.

Pre-1850s

Trade and Nomadism

The Yerukula were grain and salt traders who travelled with their families across the Madras Presidency, loading goods on donkeys and bullocks and supplying essentials from the coast to interior areas. They also dealt in forest and agricultural produce.

1850s

Railways Replace Pack Animals

When the British introduced road and railway transport in the 1850s, wagons replaced donkeys and the Yerukula lost their traditional means of livelihood. Enclosures of forest land cut them off from common pastures, and they were forced into petty theft and eventually dacoity.

1871–1911

The Criminal Tribes Act

The British colonial government enacted the Criminal Tribes Act, and the Yerukula were branded a “hereditary criminal tribe.” Colonial distrust was fanned by upper-caste Hindus who blamed the itinerant Yerukulas for thefts and robberies. Under the Act, entire communities were placed under police surveillance and forced into “reformatory” settlements.

1910–1913

Stuartpuram Settlement Established

Harold Stuart, the home member of the Madras government, established Stuartpuram as a rehabilitation colony for the Erukula tribe. It was one of 11 settlements, including Sitanagaram in Guntur district, Bitragunta in Nellore district, and Aziz Nagar in South Arcot district. Families from Kurnool, Cuddapah, Anantapur, Chittoor, Vijayawada, Guntur, Nellore and other areas were brought here.

1952

Denotification

After independence, the Government of India repealed the Criminal Tribes Act in 1952, removing the legal stigma of “criminal tribe.” The Yerukula were subsequently included under Scheduled Tribes in Andhra Pradesh, entitling them to constitutional protections and welfare programmes.

Today

The Struggle for Dignity Continues

Seventy years after denotification, many Yerukula families in Stuartpuram and other settlements still face social stigma, poverty and lack of livelihood opportunities. Community members have risen to positions in bureaucracy, politics, medicine and sports, but full rehabilitation remains an ongoing challenge.

Culture & Customs

◈

Language & Livelihood

The Yerukula speak a Dravidian dialect called Yerukula Basha. Traditionally, men made baskets and ropes, while women practised fortune-telling (sodi). Today, many are engaged in selling pork, making brooms, daily labour, and government or private jobs.

❖

Food Habits

Rice is the staple food. The Yerukula are non-vegetarians and historically ate fowls, pork, scaly and scaleless fish, field rats, jackals, fox, cats, mongoose, and carrion. Most men drink country liquor and toddy; many smoke and chew tobacco.

✿

Religion & Deities

The Yerukula are animistic and polytheistic, with strong Hindu influences. They worship Ellamma (their patron deity), Pochamma (goddess of smallpox), Balamma, Mahalaxmi, and Hindu gods like Hanuman, Rajanna and Mahadeva. They also revere the souls of departed ancestors.

❋

Festivals

They celebrate Hindu festivals including Sankranti, Shivaratri, Sri Ramanavami, Dasara, and Ugadi. In some areas where they are denied entry to Hindu temples, they worship stone and clay idols under trees.

⌘

Life Cycle Rituals

Birth, naming, menarche, marriage, and death are marked by specific customs. For example, after childbirth, the mother and child receive a purification bath on the eleventh day. On menarche, a girl is secluded and later purified with oil, turmeric, and neem leaves.

⚖

Kula Panchayat

The traditional council, led by the Berumonusha, settles disputes, family problems, debts, and land issues. It uses ordeals like dipping a hand in boiling oil or walking on fire to determine guilt. The guilty can be fined or excommunicated.

Marriage Customs

The Yerukula are a strict endogamous group with several exogamous patrilineages. Uncle–niece marriages and cross-cousin marriages (a man marrying his father's sister's daughter or his mother's brother's daughter) are highly preferred. Child marriages are not unusual, and some girls marry before attaining menarche.

Monogamy is the norm, though polygyny is occasionally observed. A marriage is usually arranged through negotiation and mutual consent, but elopement is also accepted. After marriage, residence is patrilocal — the couple lives with the groom's parents, often in a joint family.

The agu madu (betrothal) ceremony involves the groom's family visiting the bride's house with a new date-palm mat. The bride price is discussed and settled, and an earnest amount (traditionally eight rupees) is given to the bride's father. Liquor is distributed, and the first cup is served to the bride's father to ratify the alliance.

On the wedding day, the couple is smeared with turmeric and oil, bathed, and dressed in white. They sit facing a sanctified pot, and the marriage is symbolised by the throwing of turmeric-coloured rice (akshintalu) by parents and guests. A pusti — a necklace of black beads — is tied around the bride's neck, and toe rings are placed on her toes.

Sub-Tribes, Gotras & Surnames

The Yerukula are divided into several occupational sub-tribes, and each sub-tribe is further divided into four gotras: Kavadi, Sathubadi, Manubadi, and Mendraguthi. Every gotra has many house-names (intiperlu), which are used to identify a person's lineage.

The sub-tribes include the Dabba Yerukula (basket makers using bamboo), Eetha Pullala Yerukula (basket makers using date palm), Kunchapuri Yerukula (comb makers), Parike Muggula Yerukula (fortune tellers), Karivepaku Yerukula (curry leaf sellers), and Uppu Yerukula (salt sellers).

Communities known as Korama, Korabha, Kanimanu, and Kanihelu are also considered part of the wider Yerukula family.

Research & Fieldwork

This archive draws on published ethnographic and historical research, including the work of C. Prasad (Sri Krishna Devaraya University), D.S.R.S. Prakash and S. Suryanarayana Raju (Godavari Institute of Engineering and Technology), and the Encyclopaedia of Dravidian Tribes.

A field survey is being conducted in Srikakulam district, Andhra Pradesh, covering villages with a significant Yerukula population. The survey aims to document current social organisation, marriage practices, and the impact of modernisation on the community.

Data is collected through in-depth interviews with key informants (village heads, elders, and kula panchayat leaders), informal discussions, and participant observation, following standard ethnographic guidelines.

Names, Language & Classification

The community is written Yerukala, Erukala or Erukula. Among themselves they use the name Kurru, and the label comes from the women's traditional fortune-telling (eruka cheppadam). Sources: Endangered Alphabets, Wikipedia.

The Yerukala language is Dravidian and has its own script design, but most community members now speak Telugu day to day. Wikipedia reports a literacy rate of 48.12% for the Yerukula; check this against the latest Census ST tables before quoting it. Wikipedia: Yerukala people.

Earlier writers recorded the community under other names. Thurston (1909) described it as Korava in Castes and Tribes of Southern India, and Ramesan (1960) wrote on their nomadic movement in the article A Tribe in Andhra. Both are cited in the overview of Parthasarathy's 1988 ethnography. Book listing with overview.

The community is also reported outside Andhra Pradesh, for example in Puducherry (including Yanam), where inclusion in the Scheduled Tribe list has been demanded. Vajiram & Ravi (secondary source).

After 1952

Denotification & Its Aftermath

31 August 1952

Criminal Tribes Act repealed

India struck the Act from its statute books and the communities became known as Denotified Tribes. The date is observed as Vimukti Diwas. Some pages give 1949, so use 1952 and cite a source. Scroll.in · Wikipedia

1950s onward

Habitual Offenders Acts

Several states passed Habitual Offenders laws after the repeal. Critics argue these kept the old suspicion alive, and members of denotified communities are still disproportionately detained as suspects. Scroll.in · Wikipedia

9 March 2007

UN committee urges repeal

The UN Committee on the Elimination of Racial Discrimination asked India to repeal the Habitual Offenders Act (1952) and rehabilitate denotified and nomadic tribes. Wikipedia: Denotified Tribes

Stuartpuram

Settlement and its social reformers

The settlement, now in the Guntur region, is named after Stuart, then acting secretary to the Madras government. It was run with the Salvation Army, and activists Lavanam and Hemalata Lavanam of the Atheist Centre, Vijayawada, worked there for change. Malli Gandhi (2006), synopsis

Today

Stigma and success side by side

The Wire reports about 6,000 Erukula families in Stuartpuram, many facing the stigma of criminality, alongside doctors, engineers, bank officers and government officers from the settlement. The Wire (2022)

Quick answers

Frequently Asked Questions

Who are the Yerukula?

A Dravidian tribal community found mainly in Andhra Pradesh and Telangana, also spelled Yerukala, Erukala and Erukula. Source

Why are they called Yerukula?

The name comes from the women's traditional profession of fortune-telling (eruka cheppadam). Source

What is the Yerukula population?

2011 Census figures are often quoted as 519,337 for Andhra Pradesh, but Wikipedia flags that figure as needing a citation. Verify it in the Census of India ST tables before relying on it. Source

What is Stuartpuram?

A settlement set up under the colonial Criminal Tribes Act, home to roughly 6,000 Erukula families according to The Wire. Source

When was the Criminal Tribes Act repealed?

On 31 August 1952, though Habitual Offenders laws followed. Source

Are Korava and Kaikadi the same as Yerukula?

Several sources list Korcha, Korava and Kaikadi among the names used for the community, but treat each regional group as its own community unless a source says otherwise. Source
